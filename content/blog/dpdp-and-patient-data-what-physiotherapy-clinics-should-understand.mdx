India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP) has made many clinic owners aware, for the first time, that patient information is regulated personal data and that the clinic is responsible for how it is handled. The law is still being operationalised through rules, and this article is not legal advice — your obligations depend on your situation and you should take professional advice on them. What follows is an orientation: the vocabulary, the questions worth asking, and what to look for in the tools you use to store patient data.

## The vocabulary, briefly

- **Personal data** — information about an identifiable person. Patient names, contact details and clinical records are personal data.
- **Data fiduciary** — the entity that decides why and how personal data is processed. A clinic deciding to collect and store patient records is acting as a data fiduciary.
- **Data processor** — an entity that processes data on the fiduciary's behalf, such as the software vendor hosting your records.
- **Data principal** — the person the data is about: your patient.

The practical consequence: the clinic is responsible for the data, even when a vendor stores it. That makes the vendor's practices your concern.

## Questions to ask inside the clinic

### Why are we collecting this?

DPDP is built around collecting data for a stated purpose with consent. Clinics collect patient data to deliver care, which is a clear purpose. The questions are about everything else: are you also using contact details for marketing? Sharing with third parties? Each additional use deserves its own thought.

### Who in the clinic can see what?

Most leakage in small organisations is not a breach; it is over-access. Reception does not need to read clinical notes. A therapist at one branch may not need the full list of patients at another. Role-based access — each person sees only what their work requires — is both good practice and the simplest way to demonstrate care.

### How long do we keep it, and where?

Clinical records have retention requirements of their own, and professional bodies may set standards for them. Beyond that, think about where data physically lives (which vendor, which region), who has administrative access, and what happens to the data if you stop using a tool.

### What would we do if something went wrong?

A lost laptop, a misdirected message, a shared password. You do not need a corporate incident plan, but you should know who in the clinic would act, what they would check, and how patients would be informed if necessary.

## Questions to ask your software vendor

These are the questions a clinic owner can ask any vendor without being a technologist:

- **Is patient data encrypted in transit and at rest?** Both matter; "https" covers only the first.
- **Is access role-based, and can we configure it ourselves?** You should be able to decide who sees which branches and records.
- **Are backups automatic, and has a restore been tested?**
- **Can we export all our data, in a usable format, at any time?** This is about ownership and about your ability to leave.
- **Who at the vendor can access our data, and under what conditions?**
- **What happens to the data if we cancel?**

Prefer plain, specific answers over badges. A vendor who explains how data is protected is more useful than one who leads with certification logos you cannot verify.

## The WhatsApp question

Many clinics coordinate patient care in WhatsApp groups. It is worth noticing what that means under a data-protection lens: clinical details about identifiable patients are being shared on staff members' personal phones, in an app the clinic does not administer, with no role-based access and no clinic-controlled retention or export. That is not a legal conclusion — it is a description of why group chats are a weak place for clinical information, and why "where does patient data actually live?" is a question worth answering honestly.

## What good practice looks like for a physiotherapy clinic

- Patient data is in one system the clinic controls, not spread across sheets, chats and phones.
- Access is role-based and reviewed when staff join or leave.
- Data is encrypted, backed up automatically, and exportable.
- The clinic knows which vendors process its data and what they have committed to.
- Someone in the clinic owns these questions, even part-time.

None of this requires certification to begin. It requires deciding where the data lives and who can see it.
